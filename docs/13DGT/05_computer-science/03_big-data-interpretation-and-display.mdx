# [CS-5.3] Interpretation, Bias & Display

## Why This Matters

Processing big data correctly doesn't guarantee the *conclusions* drawn from it are fair or accurate. This topic — named directly in the spec as "interpretation and representation (bias and display)" — is about reading data critically: who is actually represented in a dataset, what a chart does and doesn't show, and how a technically correct number can still mislead.

---

## Where Bias Enters Big Data

Bias can be introduced at almost any stage of the pipeline from [Topic 2](02_big-data-generation-and-formats.mdx), before any analysis happens at all.

| Type of bias | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Selection / sampling bias** | The people in the dataset aren't representative of the wider population | A mobility report built only from users who opted in to location tracking over-represents smartphone owners who chose to enable that setting |
| **Opt-in bias** | Only some users have the feature that generates the data turned on | If a setting is off by default, the dataset skews toward more tech-engaged users who actively turned it on |
| **Measurement bias** | The way data is collected systematically distorts it | Comparing "visits" using relative percentage change hides the true underlying numbers |
| **Coverage gaps** | Some areas or groups simply don't generate enough data to be measured | A low-population region may not meet a platform's minimum data threshold at all |

### Worked Example: A Real Coverage Gap

Google's 2020 New Zealand Community Mobility Report is built entirely from users who had the **Location History** setting turned on — which is **off by default**. This means the underlying sample is not the whole population, but specifically the subset of people who chose to enable a particular privacy setting. This is a textbook example of **opt-in / selection bias**: it doesn't make the report useless, but it does mean any conclusion ("New Zealanders reduced retail visits by 91%") technically describes *that opted-in group*, not the whole country.

The regional breakdown for Manawatu-Wanganui in the same report also shows a category — "Transit stations" — explicitly marked with a footnote: *"Not enough data for this date: Google needs a significant volume of data to generate an aggregated and anonymous view of trends."* This is a direct illustration of the **volume** characteristic from [Topic 1](01_big-data-characteristics.mdx) causing a real gap in what can be reported, and it's worth noting in an answer that a sensible dataset owner **withholds** a statistic rather than reporting an unreliable one.

*(Source: `91908-res-2021.pdf`, Resources E–G.)*

## Reading a Claim Critically

When you're given a statistic drawn from big data, check:

1. **Population** — who was actually measured? (everyone, or an opted-in/self-selected subset?)
2. **Baseline** — what is the number being compared against, and is that baseline itself reasonable?
3. **Aggregation level** — is this a national figure hiding large regional differences?
4. **Causation vs correlation** — does the data actually show *why* something happened, or only *that* it happened alongside something else?

## Choosing and Critiquing a Display

The same underlying data can be shown in very different ways, and the choice changes what a reader notices first.

| Display | Good for | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Line graph over time** | Showing a trend and its speed (velocity) | Can exaggerate small changes if the y-axis is zoomed in |
| **Percentage change vs baseline** | Quick comparison across categories | Hides the actual underlying numbers (a 90% drop from a small baseline may be a smaller real change than a 20% drop from a huge baseline) |
| **Regional breakdown table/map** | Showing that a national average hides local variation | Can appear more precise than the data supports, especially in low-volume regions |
| **Raw counts** | Showing true scale | Can be harder to compare across groups of very different sizes |

## Worked Example: Interpreting a Dataset's Collection

> **What key problems or issues relating to the 3 Vs can you see with the collection of a dataset like this? How might these problems or issues be addressed?**
> *(Adapted from `91908-exm-2021.pdf`, Question Three (b).)*

**How to answer well:**
- Name a specific V (e.g. **veracity**, or **volume**) and tie it to a concrete collection issue — don't just say "the data might be biased"
- For the Google mobility example: veracity/representativeness is limited because only opted-in Location History users are counted; volume is limited in low-population regions, producing "not enough data" gaps
- Suggest a realistic fix: widening the sample where possible, being transparent about who is and isn't represented, or explicitly withholding statistics below a minimum data threshold (as Google does) rather than reporting an unreliable figure

A companion question style asks you to weigh the human impact directly:

> **With reference to the resources, how do the technical capabilities and limitations of big data affect people's lives? Give examples to support your answer.**
> *(`91908-exm-2021.pdf`, Question Three (c).)*

This wants you to connect a *technical* limitation (e.g. a minimum data threshold suppressing a statistic) to a *real consequence* for a specific group (e.g. policymakers in a low-population region having less evidence available to guide a local decision than a city does).

## Key Vocabulary

- **Selection / sampling bias:** When the data collected doesn't represent the true population
- **Opt-in bias:** Skew introduced because only users who actively enabled a feature are counted
- **Baseline:** The reference point a change is measured against
- **Aggregation:** Combining individual data points into a summary statistic, which can hide variation
- **Data threshold:** A minimum volume of data required before a statistic is considered reliable enough to report
